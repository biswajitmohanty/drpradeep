## [symptoms]

- Mechanical symptoms: locking, clicking, or a joint that catches
- Persistent pain despite a normal-looking X-ray (MRI often reveals internal damage)
- A torn meniscus, labrum, or cartilage flap identified on imaging
- Recurrent swelling that is slow to settle
- Loose fragments inside the joint causing pain or blockage

## [options]

- Diagnostic arthroscopy: When symptoms and imaging don't fully add up. The joint is inspected directly and treated in the same sitting if needed.
- Meniscus repair or trimming (knee): Preserves as much healthy tissue as possible.
- Cartilage treatment: Microfracture, cartilage transfer, or other techniques for focal defects.
- Ligament reconstruction: ACL, PCL, or shoulder ligaments — performed arthroscopically in most cases.
- Shoulder and hip arthroscopy: For impingement, labral tears, and loose bodies.

## [what-to-expect]

- Most arthroscopic procedures are day-case — patients go home the same evening.
- General or regional anaesthesia depending on the joint.
- Two or three keyhole incisions (5–10 mm each) are used to introduce the camera and instruments.
- Procedure time ranges from 30 minutes to 2 hours.
- Walking is usually possible the same day, with crutches for 1–2 weeks if needed.

## [recovery]

- Days 1–7: Rest, ice, elevation, and simple range-of-motion exercises. Pain is usually manageable with oral medication.
- Weeks 2–4: Progressive strengthening. Most patients return to desk work by week 2.
- Weeks 4–8: Full return to light activity. Running and sport graduate back based on the procedure.
- Months 3–6: Full return to higher-impact sport for reconstructive procedures.
- Scars fade significantly over the first year.

## [risks]

- Infection (under 1 percent)
- Temporary joint stiffness that responds to physiotherapy
- Blood clots (very rare with short procedures and early mobilisation)
- Failure of the specific repair — for example a meniscus repair that does not heal
- Anaesthetic risks discussed individually

## [faqs]

### Is arthroscopy the same as "keyhole surgery"?

Yes. Arthroscopy refers to surgery inside a joint using a small camera (arthroscope) through tiny incisions. It is the joint-specific form of keyhole surgery.

### Will I need crutches after arthroscopy?

Depends on the procedure. A simple meniscus trim needs no crutches beyond a day or two. A meniscus repair or cartilage procedure may need 2–6 weeks of protected weight-bearing.

### Can any joint problem be fixed arthroscopically?

Most internal derangements of the knee, shoulder, hip, ankle, and wrist can. Advanced arthritis usually needs a different approach — arthroscopy is not a substitute for joint replacement in bone-on-bone arthritis.

### How long does the surgery take?

Simple procedures (30–45 minutes). Ligament reconstructions or complex repairs (90–120 minutes). Your surgeon will explain the likely duration after examining the MRI.

### When can I drive after arthroscopy?

Usually 1–2 weeks for a simple procedure and when you can perform an emergency stop comfortably. Longer for ligament reconstructions or when operating on the right (accelerator) leg.

### Will the scars be visible?

Arthroscopic scars are typically 5–10 mm and fade substantially over 6–12 months. Most patients find them barely noticeable after a year.
