## [symptoms]

- Sudden severe pain and swelling after a fall, sports tackle, or road accident
- An obviously deformed limb or one that cannot bear weight
- A joint that has dislocated (shoulder, patella, elbow)
- An open wound over a suspected fracture (a surgical emergency)
- Numbness or loss of circulation beyond the injury — always urgent

## [options]

- Non-operative treatment: Many simple fractures heal with a plaster cast or functional brace and clinic follow-up.
- Closed reduction: Realigning a displaced fracture or dislocation under sedation or anaesthesia, then splinting.
- Internal fixation: Plates, screws, or intramedullary nails to stabilise displaced or unstable fractures.
- External fixation: A temporary frame for severe open fractures or when soft tissues are too swollen for immediate surgery.
- Replacement: Some elderly hip fractures and certain shoulder fractures are best treated with a joint replacement from the outset.

## [what-to-expect]

- Initial assessment in the emergency department: history, examination, X-rays, and pain control.
- CT imaging for complex fractures to plan surgery accurately.
- Urgent surgery for open fractures, dislocations with neurovascular compromise, or certain hip fractures in the elderly.
- Most trauma surgery takes 45–120 minutes. Anaesthesia is selected case by case.
- Clear plans for weight-bearing, rehabilitation, and follow-up X-rays are given before discharge.

## [recovery]

- Weeks 1–2: Pain control, wound care, and protected movement under physiotherapy.
- Weeks 2–6: Fracture-specific rehabilitation. Weight-bearing progression as healing allows.
- Weeks 6–12: X-ray evidence of healing usually appears. Graduated return to daily activities.
- Month 3–6: Most fractures heal clinically. Strength and confidence continue to improve.
- Beyond 6 months: Return to higher-impact activity and sport, usually without restriction.

## [risks]

- Delayed or non-union of the fracture in a small percentage of cases — may need further surgery
- Infection, especially after open fractures
- Stiffness of adjacent joints if rehabilitation is delayed
- Implant prominence occasionally requires hardware removal after healing
- Post-traumatic arthritis years later, particularly for fractures involving joint surfaces

## [faqs]

### Do all fractures need surgery?

No. Many fractures — fingers, toes, simple wrist fractures, some ankle fractures — heal well in a cast. Surgery is indicated when fragments are displaced, the fracture is unstable, or the joint surface is disrupted.

### How long does a fracture take to heal?

Most adult bones heal clinically in 6–12 weeks. Full strength returns over 3–6 months. Children heal faster. Location, fracture pattern, smoking, diabetes, and bone health all affect healing.

### Can I remove the plaster cast to bathe?

No — a plaster cast must stay dry. A waterproof cast cover helps. Fibreglass casts can tolerate brief splashes better but should still not be submerged. Removable functional braces allow bathing in the later stages of healing.

### Will the metal plate stay in forever?

Usually yes. Removal is only recommended if the implant causes pain, pressure against the skin, or interferes with joint movement. We do not routinely remove plates and screws.

### Why do some hip fractures get a replacement instead of fixation?

Displaced fractures of the femoral neck in older patients have poor blood supply to the ball of the hip, so fixation often fails. Replacement gets patients walking faster and more reliably.

### What should I do right after an injury while I wait to be seen?

Immobilise the limb as best you can (a rolled magazine for a forearm, a pillow splint for an ankle). Elevate if possible. Do not attempt to straighten a badly deformed joint. Head to an emergency department.
