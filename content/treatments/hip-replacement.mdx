## [symptoms]

- Groin pain that radiates to the thigh, worse with walking or standing
- Difficulty putting on socks, cutting toenails, or getting in and out of a car
- A limp that family members have started noticing
- Morning stiffness that eases with movement but returns with activity
- A fracture of the hip (often requires urgent replacement in older patients)

## [options]

- Non-surgical management: Physiotherapy, weight loss, and pain control work well for early arthritis. Most patients try these first.
- Medical therapy: Short courses of anti-inflammatories, occasional injections where appropriate.
- Hemiarthroplasty: Replaces only the ball (femoral head). Used mainly for certain fractures in older patients.
- Total hip replacement: Both the ball and socket are replaced. The standard procedure for arthritis.
- Robotic-assisted hip replacement: Sub-millimeter implant positioning using 3D planning and robotic guidance. Especially useful for complex anatomy or young patients expecting their implant to last decades.

## [what-to-expect]

- Pre-op assessment includes a full-body medical check, X-rays, and sometimes a CT scan for planning.
- Most hips are performed under spinal anaesthesia with a light sedative — patients recover more quickly than under general.
- The worn joint surfaces are removed and replaced with metal, ceramic, or polyethylene components.
- Incision length is typically 10–14 cm. Theatre time is usually 60–90 minutes.
- Hospital stay: 2–4 days for most uncomplicated cases.

## [recovery]

- Day 1: Sit up, stand, and take a few steps with support.
- Week 1: Walking with a frame or two crutches. Basic self-care regained.
- Weeks 2–6: Gradual return to longer walks, stair climbing, and sedentary work. Driving around week 4–6.
- Month 3: Most patients are pain-free and returning to full daily activity.
- Month 6 onwards: Full recovery. Light sports often resumed. Modern hip implants last 20+ years in the vast majority of patients.

## [risks]

- Infection (under 1 percent with modern sterile technique)
- Dislocation in the first 6 weeks — minimised by careful implant positioning and patient education
- Leg length discrepancy — usually small and corrected with time or a small heel raise
- Blood clots — reduced by blood thinners and early mobilisation
- Implant loosening over decades (revision surgery may be needed eventually)

## [faqs]

### When is hip replacement the right decision?

When hip pain significantly limits daily activities and non-surgical treatment has not helped over 3–6 months. In older patients with a displaced hip fracture, replacement is often the best option from day one.

### How does robotic hip replacement help?

It helps place the implant more accurately — particularly the cup position — which reduces dislocation risk, equalises leg length better, and is linked to longer implant survival in published studies.

### Can I sleep on my side after hip replacement?

Yes, usually after 4–6 weeks and once your surgeon confirms it. A pillow between your knees is helpful in the early weeks.

### Will I set off airport metal detectors?

Modern hip implants sometimes do. Your clinic can provide a card confirming the implant, but usually a brief scan resolves it.

### How long before I can drive?

Usually 4–6 weeks for a right hip replacement and 2–3 weeks for a left (automatic transmission). You must be off strong painkillers and be able to do an emergency stop.

### Is the surgery painful?

Modern pain protocols — spinal anaesthesia, local infiltration, and multimodal medication — mean most patients describe pain as well-controlled. Most are surprised by how much easier it is than they feared.
