## [symptoms]

- Neck or back pain that radiates into the arm or leg
- Numbness, tingling, or weakness in a limb
- Shoulder pain that stops you sleeping on one side
- Knee or hip pain that has lasted more than 6 weeks
- Stiffness and loss of range in multiple joints (may point to inflammatory arthritis)

## [options]

- Accurate diagnosis first: Clinical examination, targeted X-ray, MRI, or blood tests where indicated. Many back and neck problems do not need imaging at all.
- Structured physiotherapy: The single most effective treatment for most spine and joint pain. Not generic exercises — specific to the diagnosis.
- Ergonomic and activity review: Desk setup, sleeping posture, load management. Small changes make large differences.
- Targeted injections: Image-guided corticosteroid or anaesthetic injections for specific, well-localised problems.
- Surgical options: Discussed only when conservative care fails and imaging shows a problem surgery can reliably fix.

## [what-to-expect]

- A 30–45 minute first consultation: your story, a full examination, and a clear explanation of what is likely causing your pain.
- Any imaging requested is explained — including when a scan is not needed.
- A written plan you can take to your physiotherapist, including red flags to watch for.
- Follow-up appointments spaced to give your treatment time to work.
- Referral to spine specialists or rheumatologists when the condition falls outside general orthopaedic practice.

## [recovery]

- Weeks 1–2: Pain education, activity modification, and initial physiotherapy.
- Weeks 2–6: Most mechanical back and neck pain improves significantly with consistent rehab.
- Month 3: Formal reassessment. If still symptomatic, imaging and injection options are reconsidered.
- Month 6: Most patients with mechanical spine and joint pain are much better. Persistent cases are investigated thoroughly.
- Long-term: Ongoing strength and movement habits protect against recurrence.

## [risks]

- Risks of medical management are minimal but include medication side effects.
- Injection risks include temporary pain flare, infection (rare), and occasional failure to help.
- Surgery — when indicated — carries its own specific risks discussed in detail before any decision.
- The main risk of not seeking care is progression: early treatment of mechanical pain is more effective than late.

## [faqs]

### Do I need an MRI for back pain?

Usually not. Most back pain is mechanical and improves with structured rehab. MRI is reserved for pain with leg symptoms, suspected serious causes, or pain that has not responded to 6 weeks of good treatment.

### Is my pain from the spine or the hip?

Both can cause groin or thigh pain, and they sometimes coexist. A careful examination — not just a scan — usually identifies which joint is the main problem. We use selective injections occasionally when the diagnosis is unclear.

### Are steroid injections safe?

Yes, in controlled doses and not too frequently. They are a useful diagnostic and therapeutic tool for specific problems. Repeated injections into weight-bearing joints are avoided because they can weaken cartilage and tendons over time.

### When does neck or back pain become an emergency?

Pain with loss of bladder or bowel control, progressive weakness in a limb, saddle numbness, or severe pain after a high-energy injury. These need urgent evaluation.

### Will I need surgery for a disc problem?

Most disc-related pain resolves with time and rehab. Surgery is considered for severe or progressive weakness, loss of function, or pain that has failed all reasonable non-surgical options.

### Can lifestyle changes really help chronic joint pain?

Yes. Weight management, sleep, strength training, and reducing inflammatory triggers in diet all help. This is not a replacement for medical care — it is the foundation that makes medical care work better.
