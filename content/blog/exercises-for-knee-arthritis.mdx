Patients with early or moderate knee arthritis often ask me what exercises they should be doing. The internet will offer you thousands of videos — many of them contradictory, some confidently wrong. Here's the short version based on the evidence and what I see working in practice.

The goal of exercise in knee arthritis is not to "wear out" or "restore" cartilage — cartilage doesn't regenerate with exercise. The goal is to strengthen the muscles around the knee, improve joint mechanics, reduce pain, and preserve function. For most patients, a consistent, well-chosen exercise programme does as much as any medication.

## What "good" looks like

Before the list, three principles:

1. **Consistency beats intensity.** 20 minutes most days is better than 90 minutes once a week.
2. **Discomfort during exercise is OK; sharp pain is not.** A 0–10 scale: up to 3 is fine, 5+ means stop.
3. **Progress gradually.** Add repetitions or light resistance every 1–2 weeks, not daily.

## 5 exercises that help

### 1. Straight leg raises

Sit on a chair or lie on your back. Keep one knee bent, the other straight. Slowly raise the straight leg to the height of the bent knee, hold for 2 seconds, lower under control. Three sets of 10–15 on each side.

Why it works: strengthens the quadriceps (the key stabiliser of the knee) without putting the knee joint through its range of motion. Safe even during flares.

### 2. Wall sits — partial

Stand with your back against a wall, feet 30–40 cm from the wall. Slide down until your knees are bent about 30–45 degrees — not deep. Hold for 10–30 seconds, stand up, rest. Three to five repetitions.

Why it works: builds quadriceps endurance — the muscle that protects the knee during walking and stairs. The shallow bend keeps joint load manageable.

### 3. Step-ups on a low step

Use a single stair or a sturdy 15 cm platform. Step up with the weaker leg, bring the other leg up, step down the same way. Three sets of 10 each side, slow and controlled. Hold a rail or counter for balance if needed.

Why it works: functional strength for the specific movement most patients find hardest — climbing stairs. Improves quadriceps and hip control together.

### 4. Stationary cycling — light resistance

Ten to twenty minutes of easy cycling on a stationary bike, with the seat high enough that your knee doesn't fully bend at the bottom of the pedal stroke. Aim for a light cadence, not a workout.

Why it works: moves the knee through its range of motion without weight-bearing impact. Improves joint nutrition and reduces stiffness. Particularly good first thing in the morning.

### 5. Hip strengthening — side-lying leg lifts

Lie on your side. Keep the top leg straight and lift it 30–40 cm, slowly. Three sets of 10–15 each side.

Why it works: strong hip abductors reduce knee strain during walking. Weak hips are a surprisingly common contributor to knee pain. This is often the exercise that makes the biggest difference for patients whose knees hurt mainly during walking.

## 3 exercises that often make things worse

### 1. Deep squats

Full squats — knees bending past 90 degrees — put very high load on the kneecap and the front of the knee joint. In arthritic knees, this is usually painful during, and often sore the next day. Shallow squats or wall sits give you the strengthening benefit without the joint load.

### 2. Running on hard surfaces

If you're a lifelong runner with mild arthritis, gentle running on forgiving surfaces may still be fine — speak to your surgeon. For most patients with moderate knee arthritis who've been sedentary or who run on roads, running aggravates the knee more than it helps. Swimming, cycling, or elliptical work deliver the cardiovascular benefit without the repetitive impact.

### 3. Lunges — particularly forward lunges with deep bend

Lunges combine the load of a deep squat with added instability. In arthritic knees, they often trigger kneecap pain and flares of effusion. If you like lunge-style movements, very shallow "stationary split squats" holding a rail are a gentler alternative.

## A few extra thoughts

**Warm up first.** Five minutes of easy cycling or walking before your exercises reduces the stiffness that often makes the first few repetitions painful.

**Don't push through a flare.** If your knee is visibly swollen, hot, or more painful than usual, switch to gentle movements — straight leg raises, cycling — and skip the loading exercises for a few days. Ice and rest; resume when the flare settles.

**Quadriceps strength is the single most studied predictor of function in knee arthritis.** If you only have time for one exercise, make it straight leg raises or wall sits.

**Weight matters.** For patients carrying extra weight, every kilogram lost reduces forces across the knee by 3–4 kg with each step. Combined with strengthening, weight reduction is the most effective non-surgical intervention we have.

**A physiotherapist is worth the investment.** One or two sessions with a good physiotherapist — specifically to assess your movement, identify which muscles are weak, and tailor the exercises — is far more effective than a generic list from the internet (including this one). These are general starting points; an individual plan is always better.

## When exercise isn't enough

If you've been doing structured exercise properly for 3 months and your pain hasn't improved, it may be time to reassess. That doesn't automatically mean surgery — there are other options including injections, weight management support, and medication review. But it does mean a proper orthopaedic consultation is reasonable.

The goal of any knee arthritis programme is to keep you doing the things that matter to you, for as long as possible. For most patients in the early and moderate stages, exercise does exactly that. It's worth the effort.
