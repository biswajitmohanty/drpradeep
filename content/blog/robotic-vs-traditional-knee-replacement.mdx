Robotic-assisted knee replacement is one of the most hyped — and most misunderstood — developments in orthopaedics. If you're researching knee replacement in 2026, you'll see claims on one end that robotic technique is a revolutionary leap, and on the other that it's a marketing gimmick that mainly raises the bill. Neither is accurate.

This is an honest comparison, based on the published evidence and what I see in my own patients.

## What actually differs between the two

In a traditional knee replacement, the surgeon uses mechanical jigs — specially designed guides clamped to the bone — to make the cuts that prepare the femur and tibia for the implant. The alignment is checked by eye, by feel, and by physical instruments. Skilled surgeons get this right most of the time. Small errors, however, are hard to avoid: the average published error in implant alignment with conventional technique is 2–3 degrees in one or more planes.

In a robotic-assisted knee replacement, the process is different:

1. A pre-operative CT scan is used to build a 3D model of your knee.
2. The implant size, position, and alignment are planned in software, down to the millimeter.
3. During surgery, a robotic arm is registered to your actual bone positions.
4. The surgeon makes every cut while holding the robotic arm — which will not allow the cutting instrument to move outside the planned boundary.

The robot does not cut the bone. The surgeon does. The robot constrains the surgeon's movement so that the execution matches the plan.

## What the evidence shows

After 10+ years of clinical use and several thousand published patients, a reasonably clear picture has emerged:

**Implant alignment is more accurate.** Randomised trials consistently show better implant position with robotic technique — typically sub-millimeter and within 1 degree of the plan, compared to 2–3 degrees with conventional jigs. This is the most robust finding.

**Early functional recovery is modestly faster.** Several studies (including meta-analyses in the *Journal of Arthroplasty* and *Bone & Joint Journal*) show patients recover walking, stairs, and range of motion slightly faster in the first 6–12 weeks with robotic technique. The difference is real but small — on the order of days to 1–2 weeks.

**Long-term outcomes are similar or slightly better.** At 5–10 years, patient-reported outcomes and implant survival are either comparable or modestly better with robotic technique. It's too early for 20-year data on current-generation robots.

**Early revision rates are lower.** Some registry data shows a small reduction in the need for early revision surgery with robotic technique — likely because better alignment means fewer implants loosening early.

**Pain, blood loss, and length of stay** are similar, with robotic sometimes slightly better but not always.

## What robotic technique doesn't do

Some claims in marketing materials are not supported by evidence:

- It does not eliminate pain after surgery. You still have a knee replacement. It will hurt for weeks.
- It does not make implants last "forever." Implant longevity depends on many things — patient weight, activity, implant design, and alignment. Better alignment helps, but it's one factor.
- It does not avoid the need for rehabilitation. You still need physiotherapy. You still need to do your exercises.
- It does not mean the surgery is shorter. Robotic cases typically take 10–15 minutes longer because of setup and registration.

The robot is a precision tool. It improves one specific, important thing — implant placement accuracy. It doesn't change the fundamentals of what knee replacement is and what recovery involves.

## Where robotic technique helps most

Not every patient gains equally from robotic technique. It helps most in:

**Complex anatomy.** Patients with significant deformity (severe bow-legs, knock-knees, previous fractures) are harder to align accurately with conventional jigs. Robotic planning based on CT imaging handles these reliably.

**Younger patients.** A 55-year-old getting a knee replacement will likely have the implant for 25–35 years. Small improvements in alignment have compound effects over that time. For older patients whose implants need to last 10–15 years, the difference is smaller.

**Revision surgery.** Doing a second knee replacement on a knee that already has an implant in it is harder than the first. Robotic planning and execution give an additional margin of safety.

**Patients who want the most accurate result.** Even in straightforward cases, some patients are comfortable paying for a technique that is measurably more accurate. That's a valid choice.

## Where traditional technique is still a sensible choice

Robotic technique isn't the right answer for every patient:

**Straightforward cases in older patients.** For a patient in their seventies with standard anatomy, conventional knee replacement by an experienced surgeon has been shown to work well for decades. The additional cost of robotic technique may not translate into proportional benefit.

**Centres without robotic experience.** A robot is only as good as the team using it. Conventional surgery by a surgeon who does 200 cases a year is almost certainly better than robotic surgery by someone who does 20. Volume and training matter more than the specific technology.

**Cost-constrained situations.** The price difference is real. If paying for robotic technique means delaying the surgery or stretching your budget painfully, traditional surgery by a good surgeon is not a compromise — it's a reasonable choice.

## The honest bottom line

Robotic-assisted knee replacement is a real improvement over conventional technique in the specific things it's designed to improve: implant position, consistency of outcomes, and probably long-term implant survival. It is not a revolution. It is an incremental, evidence-based improvement that matters most to specific groups of patients.

For patients choosing between the two, the question I'd suggest is: "Is the surgeon experienced, is the team good, and does the technique offered fit my specific case?" That matters more than whether the letters "robotic" are in the name of the procedure.

If you're considering knee replacement, a consultation — with weight-bearing X-rays and a discussion of your specific joint, anatomy, age, and goals — will give you a clearer answer than any article can.
