"How long before I can walk properly again?" is the question I hear most often from patients considering knee replacement. The honest answer has two parts: what you'll feel after the surgery, and what's actually happening inside the knee. They don't always match.

Here's a week-by-week picture of what recovery looks like for most patients who have robotic-assisted total knee replacement, based on what I see in clinic and what published evidence suggests.

## The first 24 hours

You'll be standing within a few hours of surgery. Most of my patients take a few steps with a walking frame and a physiotherapist the same day. The knee is swollen and sore, but a combination of spinal anaesthesia, local infiltration of the joint with long-acting anaesthetic, and multimodal painkillers keeps pain manageable for almost everyone.

You'll probably sleep poorly the first night — that's normal. The ward team will check pain control and swelling through the night. Blood clot prevention starts the evening of surgery: a low-dose blood thinner, compression stockings, and keeping your ankles moving.

## Days 2 to 4 — hospital discharge

By the second day, most patients are walking to the bathroom with a frame. Day 3 or 4 is typical discharge day. Before you leave, we make sure you can:

- Walk 30–50 metres with a frame or crutches
- Climb and descend a few stairs safely
- Bend the knee to 80–90 degrees (enough to sit in a normal chair)
- Perform the home exercises your physiotherapist has taught you

Pain is controlled with oral painkillers by this point. You'll have a follow-up scheduled at 10–14 days for wound check and suture removal if needed.

## Weeks 1 to 2 — home, cautious mobility

The first two weeks at home are the most restrictive. You'll walk with a frame indoors and crutches outside. Daily tasks take longer than usual. Expect:

- Swelling that's worse at the end of the day — elevation and ice help
- A knee that feels stiff first thing in the morning
- Moments where the knee "locks" or catches briefly — this settles as swelling reduces
- Gradual reduction of pain medication

Your physiotherapist visits or sees you in clinic two or three times a week. Daily exercises at home are non-negotiable — they're the single biggest predictor of how your knee will bend long-term.

## Weeks 2 to 6 — the inflection point

This is when things accelerate. Most patients notice:

- Walking further, more confidently
- Transitioning from frame to one crutch to no aid by week 4–6
- Knee bend improving from 90 to 110+ degrees
- Sleeping through the night
- Driving again around week 4–6 (once off strong painkillers and able to perform an emergency stop)

If you work a desk job, many patients return part-time around week 4. Manual or on-your-feet jobs typically wait until 8–12 weeks.

Robotic-assisted technique often helps this phase: because the implant is placed accurately, soft tissues are under less strain during recovery, and stiffness is less of an issue in my experience. The published literature is consistent with this: a 2022 meta-analysis in *The Bone & Joint Journal* found faster functional recovery in the first 6–12 weeks with robotic-assisted technique compared to conventional.

## Months 3 to 6 — real life resumes

By month three, most patients are:

- Walking unaided for 30+ minutes
- Climbing stairs normally
- Returning to most daily activities including housework and light gardening
- Back at full-time work in sedentary or light roles
- Starting gentle swimming or stationary cycling

The knee still feels "different" from your natural knee, and activities like kneeling, squatting deep, or running remain uncomfortable. Swelling persists at the end of active days — usually until month 6.

This is also when you'll have a clinical and imaging review. Most patients discharge from regular follow-up around 6 months unless there are specific concerns.

## Months 6 to 12 — the long tail

Full recovery is a 12-month journey. The knee continues to improve well into the second half of the year, but the gains are smaller and more gradual. By 12 months, you should expect:

- Pain-free walking for hours
- Confidence in going up and down stairs, including curbs and irregular surfaces
- Return to activities like light hiking, golf, doubles tennis, and cycling
- Minimal swelling at the end of most days

A small percentage of patients — perhaps 10–15 percent — continue to have some residual discomfort, particularly around the kneecap or at the scar. For most of these, it's mild and doesn't limit daily life.

## What robotic technique actually changes

Robotic-assisted knee replacement doesn't rewrite the recovery timeline. What it reliably improves is implant position — which correlates with fewer soft-tissue problems, less strain on the surrounding ligaments, and longer implant survival over decades.

In my patients, I see:

- Slightly faster return to walking unaided in the first 6 weeks
- Less variability in how patients recover — fewer outliers with stiff knees or ongoing pain
- Higher patient-reported function scores at 6 and 12 months

The trade-off: robotic surgery takes 10–15 minutes longer in theatre, and adds cost. For the right patient — particularly someone younger who expects their implant to last 20+ years — that trade-off is usually worth it.

## The single biggest predictor of good recovery

It isn't robotic versus conventional. It isn't implant brand. It isn't your age or weight within reasonable limits. The single biggest predictor of a good recovery is how consistently you do your rehab exercises in the first three months. Patients who take the physiotherapy seriously — daily, not when they remember — end up with better knees at one year, full stop.

If you're considering knee replacement, we'll talk through whether robotic or conventional is right for your specific joint during consultation. Either way, the recovery will be easier than you probably fear — and the day-to-day progress will be faster than the "12-month" timeline makes it sound.
