The day of surgery is one most patients approach with more anxiety than they let on. Most of what I explain in the pre-operative consultation is forgotten by the night before. So here is a calm, hour-by-hour walk-through of what actually happens on the day of your hip replacement, from arrival to the first walk.

This is for a standard elective total hip replacement at a partner hospital in Bhubaneswar. Exact timings vary.

## The evening before

Most surgeons will ask you to stop eating after midnight and stop clear fluids around 6 hours before your slot. You may be allowed water up to 2 hours before. Take prescribed medications with a sip of water only if your team has specifically cleared them.

Sleep will be patchy — that's normal. Your body will do fine in surgery regardless of how well you sleep the night before.

## Arrival at the hospital (typically 2 hours before surgery)

You'll be shown to a pre-operative area. A nurse will:

- Confirm your name, date of birth, and the procedure
- Check baseline observations — pulse, blood pressure, oxygen saturation
- Start an IV line in your arm
- Mark the operated hip with an arrow (so there's zero ambiguity in theatre)
- Fit compression stockings on both legs

You'll change into a hospital gown. You can wear glasses and hearing aids into theatre if you use them. Jewellery, dentures, and nail polish come off.

## Pre-operative ward round

Both the surgeon and the anaesthetist will come to see you before surgery. Expect:

- A final discussion of what's planned, confirming consent
- Marking of the leg to be operated
- The anaesthetist explaining the anaesthetic plan — usually spinal with sedation for hips, though some patients prefer or require general
- An opportunity to ask any last questions

Most patients feel calmer after this round. Having the surgeon and anaesthetist present, with a clear plan, makes the surgery feel real and manageable.

## Transfer to theatre

You'll be wheeled on a trolley to the operating theatre complex. The anaesthetic room is where you'll be prepared:

- Monitoring is attached — ECG stickers, blood pressure cuff, oxygen probe
- A small IV cannula is confirmed or re-sited
- For a spinal anaesthetic, you sit up on the edge of the trolley while the anaesthetist gives a small injection of local anaesthetic in your lower back, followed by the spinal medicine. You'll feel warmth and numbness spreading down your legs within a few minutes.
- Most patients are given light sedation alongside — enough to be relaxed or lightly asleep during the procedure

The transfer to theatre itself is the part that feels strangest. It passes quickly.

## During the surgery (approximately 60–90 minutes)

You won't remember this period. Your body is positioned — usually on your side for a posterior approach, or flat on your back for an anterior approach. The area is cleaned, draped, and the surgery begins.

The main steps:

1. An incision of about 10–14 cm over the hip
2. Careful dissection through muscle layers, protecting nerves and vessels
3. Removing the damaged femoral head (ball)
4. Preparing the socket (acetabulum) and fitting the cup implant
5. Preparing the top of the femur (thigh bone) and fitting the stem and new ball
6. Checking range of motion, leg length, and stability
7. Closing in layers, usually with absorbable sutures and skin glue

A long-acting local anaesthetic is infiltrated around the joint before closing — this keeps pain manageable for the first 12–18 hours.

## Recovery room (1–2 hours)

You'll wake up gradually. Most patients are vaguely aware of people speaking and the sensation of being wheeled to recovery, but have no real memory of it until about an hour later. In recovery, you'll be:

- Monitored closely — pulse, blood pressure, oxygen, pain
- Warmed with a forced-air blanket (your core temperature drops during surgery)
- Offered sips of water once fully alert
- Given additional pain medication if needed

You may feel shivery for 10–30 minutes. It settles.

## Back on the ward (afternoon)

You'll be transferred to your room on the orthopaedic ward. The afternoon typically involves:

- Gradual return of sensation in the legs (from the spinal), usually over 2–4 hours
- Light meal and plenty of fluids
- Monitoring of the wound dressing for any leakage
- Continued IV antibiotics and the first dose of blood thinner

Family members can visit once you're on the ward. Most patients feel well enough to chat for short periods but tire quickly.

## Evening — the first walk

Here's where most patients are surprised. Within 4–6 hours of surgery — often around 6–8 PM for a morning case — a physiotherapist will arrive with a walking frame. With the spinal wearing off and appropriate pain cover, you'll:

- Sit up at the edge of the bed
- Stand with the frame, weight-bearing as much as comfortable on the new hip
- Take a few steps, usually 3–10 metres
- Sit back down, safely

This first walk is always shorter than people expect. It's also always a huge psychological lift — the realisation that the hip actually works, that you can bear weight on it, and that the surgery is done.

## Overnight

You'll be given pain medication on a schedule rather than "when needed" — this keeps pain well ahead of the curve. Most patients sleep in short stretches of 2–3 hours the first night. Don't expect a full night's sleep. A nurse checks on you every few hours.

By morning, the physiotherapist returns for a longer walk, and the real recovery begins. Most patients are home within 3 days.

## The small things that help

A few practical suggestions from patients who've been through this:

- **Bring slip-on shoes.** Tying laces is hard for the first 4–6 weeks.
- **Arrange home in advance.** Remove loose rugs, clear paths, install a toilet seat raiser if possible.
- **Charge all your devices.** You'll be in bed a lot; having a phone, tablet, and charger nearby is a small thing that matters.
- **Eat light the evening before.** Your appetite will be subdued for 24 hours after surgery.
- **Trust the team.** Ask questions, but don't micromanage. You're in good hands.

The day of surgery is the part patients worry about most. In practice, it's a well-choreographed, well-rehearsed sequence — and by the time you take your first walk that evening, the worst of it is behind you.
