Knee pain that shows up in your fifties is common. It's also the point at which many patients start quietly worrying: *Will I need a replacement? When?* The honest answer is that most people in their fifties with knee pain don't need surgery — and shouldn't rush to it. But some do, and knowing where you fall matters.

This is a practical guide to the signs that tell me, in clinic, whether we're in "manage the pain" territory or "start thinking seriously about surgery" territory.

## Most knee pain at 50 is not the start of a replacement

Before anything else: the majority of patients I see with knee pain in their fifties have conditions that respond well to non-surgical care. Early osteoarthritis, meniscus degeneration, patellofemoral pain, and biomechanical overload from weight gain or deconditioning are all common — and all of them improve significantly with structured rehabilitation, weight management, and time.

If a physiotherapist, a sensible exercise plan, and occasional anti-inflammatory medication have not been tried properly, surgery is almost never the right first step. "Properly" means 3–6 months of consistent effort, not a week of stretches and giving up.

## The red flags that say "this is different"

A small number of findings change the picture. If you have any of these, it's worth a proper orthopaedic assessment sooner rather than later:

**Pain that wakes you up at night.** Mechanical knee pain — from overuse, early arthritis, or soft-tissue irritation — usually eases with rest. Pain that wakes you from sleep, or that aches while you're simply sitting still, points toward more advanced joint damage.

**A knee that gives way.** Feeling your knee buckle unexpectedly when you take a step, go down stairs, or turn, is a sign of either significant ligament or muscle weakness, or cartilage and bone damage that's mechanically unstable. Both need a proper diagnosis.

**Pain that doesn't respond to eight weeks of good physiotherapy.** Not generic exercises. Not "I did some stretches." A structured, progressive programme from a qualified physiotherapist, done consistently for two months. If pain hasn't improved meaningfully in that time, imaging is warranted.

**Swelling that keeps returning.** An occasional swollen knee after a long day is not alarming. A knee that's visibly swollen most of the time — or that fills up within hours of activity — is telling you something.

**Deformity that's changed.** If your legs have started to look more bow-legged or knock-kneed, and photographs from five years ago confirm the change, that's progressive arthritis doing its work.

## What a good assessment looks like

If you reach an orthopaedic clinic with one or more of these red flags, a thorough assessment should include:

- A detailed history — when the pain started, what makes it better or worse, what you've already tried
- A physical examination of both knees (not just the painful one) and the hip, ankle, and spine
- Weight-bearing X-rays of both knees in standing position — *not* lying down, because lying down often hides the joint space narrowing we need to see
- MRI *only* if the X-rays and examination suggest a soft-tissue problem that would change management

You should leave the consultation with a clear answer to three questions: what's likely wrong, what's the range of realistic outcomes, and what's the honest order of treatments we'll try.

## The spectrum of options before surgery

Even if you have proven osteoarthritis, there's a lot of road before surgery. In order of typical progression:

1. **Weight management.** Losing 5–10 percent of your body weight can reduce knee symptoms by 50 percent or more. This is the single most effective non-surgical intervention. It's also the hardest.
2. **Targeted physiotherapy.** Strengthening the quadriceps, hamstrings, and hip muscles that stabilise the knee. Generic squats won't do it — specific, diagnosis-led exercises will.
3. **Anti-inflammatory medication.** Short courses for flares. Not daily, long-term use — the gastric and kidney risks of chronic NSAID use are real.
4. **Injections.** A single corticosteroid injection can buy 3–6 months of relief for an inflamed knee. Hyaluronic acid injections help some patients with mild-to-moderate arthritis, though the evidence is mixed. Repeated injections aren't harmless.
5. **Activity modification.** Swapping high-impact activity for cycling, swimming, or elliptical work preserves the knee while keeping you fit.
6. **Joint-preserving surgery.** For specific patterns — a torn meniscus with mechanical symptoms, a localised cartilage defect, or malalignment — arthroscopy or osteotomy can postpone replacement by years.

Only once these have been tried meaningfully — or when imaging and symptoms together show it's pointless to continue — does replacement enter the conversation.

## When replacement is the right answer

Here's how I phrase it to patients: replacement is the right answer when the knee is limiting the life you want to live, and when non-surgical options have reached their ceiling.

Concretely, that usually looks like:

- Pain that's stopped you doing things you previously enjoyed — walking, working, playing with grandchildren
- Weight-bearing X-rays showing advanced joint space narrowing or bone-on-bone changes
- A consistent pattern of pain despite proper conservative care for 6+ months
- A patient who understands the procedure, the recovery, and the long-term trade-offs

Age, on its own, is rarely the deciding factor. A 55-year-old with a severely damaged knee and an active life may benefit more from replacement than a 72-year-old whose arthritis is manageable with rehab and occasional injections.

## The thing most patients don't expect

The surprise isn't usually how much pain there is before surgery — it's how much pain people tolerate before they agree to it. I regularly see patients who've been limiting their lives for 5, 10, sometimes 15 years because surgery "felt too big." Modern knee replacement, particularly robotic-assisted, is a predictable, reliable procedure with a recovery that's faster than most people imagine.

If you're in your fifties with knee pain, the first thing to do is rule in or rule out the common, non-surgical causes. If conservative care isn't working, don't wait years hoping it will. An orthopaedic consultation doesn't commit you to anything — it just gives you clarity about where you actually stand.
